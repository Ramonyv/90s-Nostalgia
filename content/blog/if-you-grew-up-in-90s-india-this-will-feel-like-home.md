There was a time when **"I'm outside" actually meant someone was standing outside your house.**

No live location. No blue ticks. No family WhatsApp group. No notification telling you that you had a memory to look back on.

We were simply living it.

And perhaps that is why those memories feel so powerful today.

The strange thing about growing up is that nobody tells you which ordinary day will become the one you desperately want to visit again.

That boring summer afternoon. That neighbourhood shop. That train window. That Sunday television programme. That cassette you rewound with a pencil. That barber shop your father took you to.

At the time, none of it felt historic.

It was just **home**.

---

## We Didn't Take Photos of Everything

Today, a good meal gets photographed. A trip gets documented. A birthday becomes a hundred photos, Stories and videos.

But many of our strongest childhood memories have almost no photographic evidence.

Maybe there are twelve photographs from an entire summer vacation. Some are blurry. Someone's eyes are closed. Half the family is looking somewhere else.

And somehow, those photographs are priceless.

Because the memory wasn't created for the camera.

**The camera was lucky to be there.**

---

## ₹10 Could Create an Entire Evening

You didn't need much.

A cold drink. A packet of chips. A tennis ball shared between six children. Maybe an orange ice candy from the cart outside.

Then someone shouted:

**"Batting meri."**

And suddenly the street became a stadium.

The electricity pole was the boundary. A brick was the wicket. The neighbour's window was an automatic out.

Every neighbourhood had its own rules. Every argument felt like an international controversy.

And when someone's mother finally shouted their name from the balcony, the match was over.

Not because the game had finished.

Because **ghar jaane ka time ho gaya tha.**

---

## The Television Was a Meeting Place

There was usually one television. And somehow, that was enough.

Families gathered. Someone adjusted the antenna. Someone complained about the signal. Someone sat too close to the screen.

When a favourite programme started, the room changed.

The television wasn't background noise. It was an **event**.

You couldn't pause it. You couldn't watch it whenever you wanted.

If you missed it, you missed it.

Maybe anticipation made everything more valuable.

---

## Music Had Weight

Before thousands of songs lived inside our pockets, music occupied physical space.

Cassettes sat in drawers, racks and glove compartments. They had handwritten labels: Side A. Side B. Favourite songs recorded from somewhere else.

Sometimes the tape got tangled. Sometimes the player ate it.

And sometimes a pencil became advanced repair technology.

Choosing music required effort, which meant we often listened to the same album again and again.

Years later, the first few seconds of one song can suddenly transport us back to a room that no longer exists.

---

## Train Journeys Started Before the Train Arrived

A railway journey wasn't simply transportation.

The excitement began at home.

Water bottles filled. Food packed. Bags counted repeatedly.

Someone inevitably asked:

**"Ticket rakha hai na?"**

Then came the station: announcements echoing overhead, steel benches, vendors moving through crowds and that unmistakable railway-platform atmosphere.

And then the greatest prize of childhood travel:

**the window seat.**

Fields passed. Tiny stations appeared and disappeared. People waved at trains for no particular reason.

For a few hours, India became a moving film outside the window.

There was nowhere else you needed to be.

---

## Summer Vacation Felt Almost Infinite

The final school bell before summer vacation felt like freedom itself.

Days suddenly became enormous.

There were cousins. Mangoes. Ceiling fans. Afternoon naps adults insisted upon and children resisted. Comics. Cricket. Trips to grandparents.

Power cuts forced everyone outside.

Nobody called it "digital detox."

There was simply nothing to detox from.

Boredom existed. And boredom was surprisingly useful.

It made us invent games, stories, competitions and entire worlds.

---

## Even Getting a Haircut Was a Memory

The neighbourhood salon had its own atmosphere.

A rotating chair that felt enormous when you were small. A mirror reflecting another mirror. Old film-star hairstyles on the wall. The sharp sound of scissors near your ears. Talcum powder. A brush across the neck.

Your father saying:

**"Chhota kar do."**

You silently hoping the barber understood that this instruction was negotiable.

It was never just a haircut.

It was one of those tiny rituals of growing up.

---

## We Were Waiting for the Future

This may be the funniest part.

Back then, we wanted everything we have now.

Faster internet. More television channels. Better games. Instant music. Mobile phones. Computers everywhere.

We imagined the future would be incredible.

And in many ways, it is.

But nobody warned us that once the future arrived...

**we would occasionally want the old world back.**

Not because everything was better. It wasn't.

But because *we were there.*

Our parents were younger. Grandparents were around. Friends lived a few houses away. Summer felt longer.

And life hadn't yet shown us how quickly places, people and routines disappear.

---

## Maybe Nostalgia Isn't About the 90s

Maybe we're not really missing cassette tapes, CRT televisions, landline phones or old railway coaches.

Maybe those objects are simply doors.

We see one and suddenly remember the house, the voices, the smell of dinner, the friend we haven't spoken to in twenty years, the person who used to sit beside us, and the version of ourselves who thought all of this would last forever.

That's what nostalgia does.

**It hides people inside objects.**

## That's Why I Built 90s Yaadein

I didn't want to make another gallery of old photographs.

I wanted to see whether a memory could become a **place you could enter again**.

A rainy window. A railway journey. A neighbourhood salon. A cassette shop. An evening at home. A street where children are still playing cricket.

So I started building **90s Yaadein** — an interactive archive of everyday India.

Not a historically perfect reconstruction. Not a museum.

Something more emotional.

A small portal to places that exist somewhere between memory and imagination.

Every scene uses illustration, interaction, sound and motion to try to recreate a feeling that photographs alone sometimes cannot.

Because some memories aren't meant to be looked at.

**They're meant to be felt.**

## Before You Leave...

There is probably one tiny thing from your childhood that you haven't thought about in years.

Maybe it's the sound of your school bell. The smell of a new textbook. A particular shop near your house. Your grandfather's radio. A biscuit you dipped into tea. A song playing from another room.

Or someone calling your childhood nickname from downstairs.

You don't have to remember everything.

Sometimes one small detail is enough.

And suddenly...

**you're home again.**
