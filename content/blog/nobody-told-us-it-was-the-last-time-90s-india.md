At some point in your childhood, **you went downstairs to play for the last time.**

Nobody announced it.

There was no final match. No photograph. No goodbye.

Someone probably shouted, **"Kal milte hain."**

You went upstairs because it was getting dark.

The bat was left behind a door. The cycle went into the building. Your mother asked you to wash your hands before dinner.

And life continued.

Except the exact version of your world that existed that evening **never assembled again.**

You just didn't know it yet.

---

## Childhood Rarely Ends on a Big Day

We imagine endings as dramatic things.

The last day of school. A farewell. Moving to another city.

But most of childhood disappeared quietly.

There was a last time you rang your friend's doorbell and asked:

**"Aunty, woh neeche aayega?"**

A last time six people somehow fitted on one bed during a cousin's summer visit.

A last time you fell asleep in the back seat and woke up inside your house because your father carried you in.

A last time your mother called your name from the balcony because dinner was ready.

Nobody wrote down the dates.

That is what makes them hurt a little.

---

## The Last Street-Cricket Match

Think about your old team.

Not the official one.

The one assembled from whoever happened to be downstairs.

The older kid owned the good bat. Someone always wanted first batting. One child was permanently sent to retrieve the ball.

The rules belonged only to your street:

**One-tip-one-hand out.**

**Direct building mein gaya toh six.**

**Sharma uncle ke ghar gaya toh jisne maara woh lekar aayega.**

You argued like professional athletes and forgot the argument ten minutes later.

Then, gradually, things changed.

Tuition became serious.

Someone moved away.

Someone got a computer.

Someone joined coaching classes.

Someone simply stopped coming downstairs.

There was no final score.

The tournament just... ended.

---

## The Last Summer When Everyone Was There

There was probably a summer when your grandparents' house was full.

Mattresses on the floor. Suitcases against walls. Steel glasses everywhere. Cousins fighting over the cooler. Adults talking long after the children were supposed to be asleep.

At the time, the house felt crowded.

You may even have wished for some privacy.

Years later, you would give almost anything to hear that noise again.

Families don't usually realise which gathering will be the last one with **exactly those people, exactly that young, in exactly that house.**

Next summer someone had exams.

Then someone had a job.

Then someone got married.

Then a house was sold.

Then one chair became permanently empty.

## The Last Cassette You Rewound

There was a time when a favourite song could require work.

Press eject.

Turn the cassette.

Press play.

Wrong song.

Fast-forward.

Too far.

Rewind.

And if the tape escaped from the cassette, find a pencil and perform surgery.

Then MP3s arrived.

CDs became easier.

Phones became smarter.

Music became infinite.

Nobody held a ceremony for the cassette player.

One day it simply stopped being the thing you reached for.

Years later, you see one in an old cupboard and your brain plays a song before the machine does.

---

## The Last Time the Landline Rang for You

Remember hearing the phone ring from another room?

Someone picked it up.

Then came the shout:

**"Tumhara phone hai!"**

And suddenly everyone in the house knew you had a call.

Privacy meant stretching the telephone wire as far as physically possible.

Sometimes a friend called.

Sometimes a relative called from another city and the entire family took turns speaking.

Then mobile phones arrived.

The landline rang less.

Eventually it became an object sitting quietly in a corner.

You probably don't remember the last call.

But there was one.

---

## The Last Evening Before the Internet Followed Us Everywhere

The old world was not magically better.

We got bored.

We waited.

We missed calls.

We got lost.

We had fewer choices.

But there were places the outside world couldn't reach us.

A rooftop at sunset.

A train window.

A power cut.

A cricket match downstairs.

A cousin's house during summer.

When you were there, **you were mostly there.**

Nobody could send you seventeen notifications while you watched the sky turn orange.

Nobody knew your location every minute.

Nobody expected an immediate reply.

Sometimes your entire social network was simply whoever was standing near the paan shop at 7 PM.

---

## Then One Day We Went Inside

Maybe this is the part that feels strange.

As children, our parents spent years calling us home.

**"Bas, ab ghar aa jao!"**

Five more minutes.

One last over.

One last round on the cycle.

One last conversation at the gate.

We always wanted to stay outside a little longer.

Then somewhere between school, college, jobs, cities and smartphones...

we went inside.

And nobody had to call us anymore.

---

## Why Do These Memories Hit Harder Now?

Because nostalgia is not really a desire to own old things.

You probably don't genuinely want dial-up internet back.

You don't need a cassette player to replace Spotify.

You don't want power cuts.

What you want is much harder to recover.

**The people around those things.**

The version of your parents who were younger.

The grandparents whose house still existed.

The friends who were always five minutes away.

The body that could play cricket for four hours and wake up fine the next morning.

The mind that could experience an ordinary Sunday without wondering whether it was productive.

The objects are only shortcuts.

A cassette. A Doordarshan sound. An old Maruti. A school notebook.

You see them and, for half a second, **everyone comes back.**

## Maybe That Is Why We Built 90s Yaadein

Not to pretend the past was perfect.

And not to escape the present.

But because some memories deserve more than a folder of old photographs.

They deserve atmosphere.

Sound.

Places.

Tiny details.

The feeling of walking into a world you thought had disappeared.

That is what **90s Yaadein** is trying to preserve: not just what India looked like, but what growing up here *felt like*.

If one scene makes you remember a person, a street, a summer, or a version of yourself you had forgotten, the experiment has worked.

---

## Before You Leave, Try to Remember One Thing

What was the last game you played downstairs?

Who was there?

What did your old house sound like in the evening?

Whose voice used to call you home?

You may not remember the date.

That's okay.

Some of the most important days of our lives passed without telling us they were important.

**We thought we were saying, "See you tomorrow."**

Sometimes, without knowing it,

**we were saying goodbye.**
