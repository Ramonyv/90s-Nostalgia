# The 90s Things We Forgot Until We Saw Them Again

There are things from childhood you remember forever.

Your school. Your best friend. Your first bicycle. The house where you grew up.

And then there are the other things.

Things so ordinary that your brain never bothered to preserve them.

The wrapper of a candy. The sound of a cheap plastic game. A cassette with the tape pulled out. The orange drink you bought because you had exactly enough coins in your pocket.

You don't spend your adult life thinking about these things.

Then, twenty-five years later, you see one.

And somehow you remember **everything around it.**

## Memory Has a Strange Door

Look at an old object from the 90s and something interesting happens.

You don't only remember the object. You remember the place.

A packet of candy can suddenly put you outside a school gate at 1:30 in the afternoon. An old handheld game can take you back to a train journey. A cassette can bring back the room where the music was playing.

You remember the furniture. The weather. The people who were there.

Sometimes you even remember who you were.

The photograph shows you the memory.

The object **opens it.**

## The ₹1 Economy of Childhood

For a 90s kid, a tiny amount of money could create an unreasonable amount of happiness.

You stood at the neighbourhood shop carefully calculating your options.

One candy now? Save for something better tomorrow? Split something with a friend?

There was an entire childhood economy operating through coins hidden in school-bag pockets.

The purchase itself was small. The ceremony around it wasn't.

Today we can order almost anything without leaving a chair. Yet somehow that tiny transaction felt more exciting.

Maybe convenience removed something we didn't know was part of the experience:

**anticipation.**

## Before Every Screen Became the Same Screen

Objects had jobs.

The television was for television. The telephone was for calling someone. The camera took photographs. The cassette player played music. The alarm clock woke you up.

Today one rectangle in our pocket does all of those things.

It is objectively extraordinary.

But it also means many objects that once populated our lives have disappeared — and with them, hundreds of tiny rituals.

Rewinding. Waiting. Dialling. Adjusting antennas. Untangling wires. Searching for batteries.

These inconveniences were annoying then.

Now they are memories.

## The Toy You Had Completely Forgotten

The strangest nostalgia is not seeing something you already remember.

It is seeing something you had **forgotten that you remembered.**

An oddly shaped spinning top. A plastic water game. A cheap pocket video game. A tiny paper toy sold outside school.

Suddenly your brain responds before you have time to think:

**“Arre… I had this!”**

For a few seconds, adulthood disappears.

Maybe your brother was beside you. Maybe your grandmother was watching television. Maybe somebody who isn't around anymore was still in the next room.

That's when nostalgia stops being about old products.

It becomes about people.

## The Background of Childhood

When we talk about the 90s, we usually remember films, songs, television shows and cricket matches.

But childhood was mostly made from background details.

The sound of a ceiling fan. A scooter starting downstairs. The smell of a new school notebook. A neighbour shouting your name from the street. A landline ringing and everyone wondering who it was for. Sunday newspapers spread across the floor. Cassette boxes stacked beside the television.

None of these things seemed important enough to photograph.

That's exactly why seeing them again feels so powerful.

## We Didn't Know Anything Was Disappearing

Nobody announced the end of these things.

There wasn't a final day of cassette tapes.

Nobody told us:

*“This is the last summer you'll spend playing outside like this.”*

The neighbourhood shop changed slowly. The television changed. The telephone disappeared. Friends moved away. Buildings were rebuilt.

We grew older.

One ordinary thing was replaced by another until, eventually, an entire world had vanished.

And because the change happened gradually, we barely noticed.

## Why 90s Yaadein Is Built Around Places, Not Lists

This is the idea behind **90s Yaadein**.

I didn't want nostalgia to become another page saying “20 things only 90s kids remember.”

I wanted to recreate the *place around the memory*.

A neighbourhood salon. A rainy evening. A train journey. A roadside dhaba. An auto-rickshaw ride.

The kind of spaces where ordinary life happened.

Because perhaps we aren't actually nostalgic for cassette tapes, old televisions or candies.

We're nostalgic for the world those things belonged to.

And sometimes all it takes is one forgotten object to find the door back.

## Before You Leave, Try This

Look at an old object from your childhood today.

Not your favourite toy. Find something completely ordinary.

An old bottle. A school badge. A cassette. A keychain. A game. A wrapper you recognize.

Then notice what your brain does next.

Don't ask:

**“Do I remember this?”**

Ask:

**“What else does this make me remember?”**

That's usually where the real memory is hiding.
